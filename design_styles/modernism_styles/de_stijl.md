## What it is

De Stijl (“The Style”) was a Dutch art and design movement associated with the journal of the same name, founded in 1917 by Theo van Doesburg. Its practitioners explored abstraction and a visual language built from horizontal and vertical lines, rectangular planes, and carefully balanced color. Piet Mondrian’s related Neo-Plasticism sought a form of visual harmony, while other De Stijl artists and architects developed their own approaches. The movement is broader than a red, yellow, and blue grid. [MoMA, de Stijl](https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/de-stijl), [MoMA, Neo-Plasticism](https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/neo-plasticism).

## Recognize and apply it

- **Orthogonal structure:** Horizontal and vertical lines divide a composition into measured areas. Use the grid to establish relationships and reading order.
- **Reduced forms:** Rectangles and flat planes take priority over representation and texture. Keep the composition deliberate so reduction does not become arbitrary.
- **Asymmetric balance:** Unequal areas can feel stable when their sizes, positions, and colors are carefully related. Test the whole composition, not only individual blocks.

For a poster or hero, begin with a grid and a small number of rectangular color fields. Let the headline occupy a deliberate area, and use alignment and spacing to carry the structure.

## Historical examples

### 1. Piet Mondrian, *Composition No. II, with Red and Blue* (1929)

- **Institution and direct source:** The Museum of Modern Art, [collection record](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/79816).
- **Image/viewing, credit, and reuse:** MoMA provides the object record and viewing image. The work is copyrighted; obtain permission before reproducing the artwork.
- **What I observe:** Black horizontal and vertical lines create unequal rectangular spaces, with red and blue areas set against white. The restraint makes the proportion and placement of each element especially visible.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** Build a modular layout from a few unequal panels, then reserve one clear panel for the headline and call to action.

### 2. Gerrit Rietveld, *Red Blue Chair* (1918–1923)

- **Institution and direct source:** The Museum of Modern Art, [collection record](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/4044/).
- **Image/viewing, credit, and reuse:** MoMA provides the object record and image. The work is copyrighted; consult MoMA’s licensing guidance before reproducing it.
- **What I observe:** Separate planes and linear supports make the chair’s construction legible. Primary colors distinguish surfaces without hiding the structural arrangement.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** Use separated planes or simple blocks to organize product information, keeping color tied to content roles rather than adding decoration.
