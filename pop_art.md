# Pop Art

[Home](README.md)

## What it is

Pop Art emerged in Britain and the United States in the 1950s and 1960s, drawing on advertising, comics, celebrity, consumer products, and other images from mass culture. Artists made familiar commercial imagery a subject for art, sometimes repeating or enlarging it to examine how popular images circulate and gain meaning. Pop Art is not simply a bright color palette; its use of popular imagery and reproduction is central. [MoMA, Pop art](https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/pop-art).

## Recognize and apply it

- **Popular imagery:** Everyday products, celebrities, comics, and mass-media references make the subject immediately recognizable. Use images you have permission to use and avoid misleading associations.
- **Graphic reproduction:** Flat color, strong outlines, halftone patterns, and screen-print-like effects recall commercial printing. Use these features to establish hierarchy, not as decoration alone.
- **Repetition and cropping:** Repeated images or bold crops draw attention to circulation, variation, or brand familiarity. Keep enough context for the audience to understand the subject.

For a campaign, make the product or message image prominent and pair it with concise copy. A bold visual reference should support the offer rather than overwhelm it.

## Historical examples

### 1. Roy Lichtenstein, *Look Mickey* (1961)

- **Institution and direct source:** National Gallery of Art, [collection record](https://www.nga.gov/artworks/71479-look-mickey).
- **Image/viewing, credit, and reuse:** The NGA record provides an image and identifies the painting as a 1961 oil on canvas. The record notes that the media is not available for download; link to the work rather than copying its image.
- **What I observe:** Lichtenstein adapts comic imagery with flat primary color, dark outlines, speech text, and dots that evoke commercial printing. Enlarging a familiar visual language makes the reproduction process itself noticeable.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** Use a newly created illustration with bold outlines and a halftone texture; do not reproduce protected comic characters or copy the painting.

### 2. Andy Warhol, *Campbell's Soup Cans* (1962)

- **Institution and direct source:** The Museum of Modern Art, [collection record](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/79809).
- **Image/viewing, credit, and reuse:** MoMA displays the 32-panel work and provides the collection record. The work is copyrighted; MoMA directs reproduction requests to its licensing partners. Link for viewing and obtain permission before reproducing the artwork.
- **What I observe:** Thirty-two varieties are presented in a repeated format resembling a grocery display. The ordinary product and serial arrangement connect consumer culture with the conventions of fine art.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** Repeat a product image in a controlled grid to highlight choice or variety, then break the pattern once to mark the featured offer.
