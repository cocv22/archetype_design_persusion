# Memphis Design

[Home](../../README.md)

## What it is

Memphis Design is a postmodern movement in furniture and product design that emerged in Milan around the Memphis group, founded by Ettore Sottsass and collaborators in 1980. The group’s 1981 debut challenged Modernist restraint and the idea that good design must be neutral, timeless, and purely functional. Memphis combined everyday materials with historic references, commercial patterns, visual jokes, and deliberately unusual forms. [Design Museum, Memphis](https://designmuseum.org/memphis), [V&A, Postmodern design](https://www.vam.ac.uk/info/collection-selection-boxes-postmodern-design).

## Recognize and apply it

- **Bold color and pattern:** Use saturated color, stripes, dots, and clashing motifs to create energy. Limit the number of competing patterns when information needs to remain easy to read.
- **Geometric, assembled forms:** Circles, cones, zigzags, and asymmetrical shapes make objects feel playful and sculptural. Use a strong silhouette to keep the composition coherent.
- **Unexpected material and scale:** Laminate, metal, plastic, and decorative surfaces can be juxtaposed without disguising their everyday character. Use contrast to make a product feel expressive, not to imply unsupported quality.

In a poster or hero, use a playful patterned field and one dominant product image. Keep the headline and call to action on a simple, high-contrast surface.

## Historical examples

### 1. Ettore Sottsass, *Carlton* room divider (1981)

- **Institution and direct source:** Design Museum, London, [Ettore Sottsass profile and collection images](https://designmuseum.org/designers/ettore-sottsass).
- **Image/viewing, credit, and reuse:** The Design Museum page shows the object and identifies it as a 1981 design. Its page credits the displayed exhibition photograph to Andy Stevens. No general reuse license is stated; link to view and seek permission before reproducing the image.
- **What I observe:** The divider works as both furniture and a large sculptural presence. Its tilted shelves and bright, patterned surfaces resist the quiet neutrality associated with conventional storage.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** Use stacked, offset color blocks around one clear product image, adapting the divider’s asymmetry without obscuring the offer.

### 2. Martine Bedin, *Super* lamp (1981)

- **Institution and direct source:** Design Museum, London, [Memphis history and object images](https://designmuseum.org/memphis).
- **Image/viewing, credit, and reuse:** The page describes the lamp and includes Memphis exhibition imagery; it credits images on the page to Dennis Zanone. No general reuse license is stated. Use the source as a viewing link and request permission before reuse.
- **What I observe:** The curved, brightly colored lamp base and repeated bulbs give a functional object a character-like presence. Utility is retained, but visual expression is foregrounded.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** Give the product a distinctive outline and repeat a small geometric motif as a secondary detail, while reserving a clean area for copy.
