# Neo-Expressionism

[Home](../../README.md)

## What it is

Neo-Expressionism describes a varied return to expressive, often figurative painting that became prominent in Europe and the United States in the late 1970s and 1980s. Artists used forceful mark-making, large scale, recognizable imagery, and layered or unconventional materials. In part, this renewed painting’s emotional and historical subjects after the more restrained approaches associated with Minimal and Conceptual art. The label covers different artists rather than a single shared style. [National Galleries of Scotland, Neo-Expressionism](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/glossary-terms/neo-expressionism), [Guggenheim Bilbao, Expressionism in the 20th century](https://www.guggenheim-bilbao.eus/en/exhibitions/the-avant-garde-and-expressionism-in-the-20th-century).

## Recognize and apply it

- **Forceful, visible gesture:** Broad brushwork and rough surfaces make the act of making part of the image. Use expressive marks with a clear focal point.
- **Figurative or symbolic imagery:** Bodies, landscapes, history, and myth return in distorted or emotionally charged forms. Choose imagery with a clear relationship to the message.
- **Material layering and scale:** Paint may be combined with found or rough materials, and compositions can feel physically imposing. In digital work, suggest texture carefully without sacrificing readability.

For a hero, use one expressive image or textured surface, then set concise copy against a quieter area. The contrast lets the artwork carry emotion while the message remains readable.

## Historical examples

### 1. Julian Schnabel, *Bones and Trumpets Rubbing Against Each Other Towards Infinity* (1981)

- **Institution and direct source:** The Metropolitan Museum of Art, [collection record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/715452).
- **Image/viewing, credit, and reuse:** The Met page provides an image and object record. It identifies the work as copyright-protected; the museum page gives no general license for this image. Link for viewing and seek permission before reuse.
- **What I observe:** Schnabel combines paint with broken ceramic, plaster, wood, and animal remains on a monumental support. The rough materials and outlined imagery make the surface feel physical and emotionally direct.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** Use a restrained, original paper or paint texture behind the product, then preserve a clear text panel rather than copying the artwork.

### 2. Anselm Kiefer, *The Unknown Masterpiece* (1982)

- **Institution and direct source:** The Metropolitan Museum of Art, [collection record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/490047).
- **Image/viewing, credit, and reuse:** The Met collection record provides the object details and image. Copyright and reproduction restrictions apply; the page does not grant general reuse permission. Link for viewing only unless permission is obtained.
- **What I observe:** Kiefer layers material and historical reference to make painting carry memory and interpretation, rather than treating surface as a neutral window. The dense construction asks the viewer to examine the work over time.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** Layer a small number of relevant visual elements to suggest depth, while ensuring the headline and CTA remain immediately findable.
