## What it is

The International Style is an architectural approach that emerged in Europe during the 1920s and 1930s. The term was popularized by Henry-Russell Hitchcock and Philip Johnson through the Museum of Modern Art’s 1932 exhibition *Modern Architecture: International Exhibition*. Its architecture favored clear volume, regular geometry, and minimal applied ornament, often using steel, reinforced concrete, and glass. The label brought together work from different countries, so it should not be treated as a single uniform style. [MoMA, International Style](https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/international-style), [MoMA, 1932 exhibition catalogue](https://www.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_2044_300061855.pdf).

## Recognize and apply it

- **Clear structure:** Volumes and supports are easy to read. Make the relationship between elements visible rather than disguising it with surface effects.
- **Regular geometry and open space:** Repeated forms and uncluttered planes create order. Preserve enough contrast and hierarchy so simplicity does not flatten the content.
- **Material honesty:** Glass, steel, and concrete are used for their structural and spatial qualities. In graphic work, apply the same clarity through grids, restrained typography, and disciplined spacing.

For a digital hero or poster, use a consistent grid, simple sans-serif type, and generous open space. Treat the grid as a tool for clarity, not a requirement that every element be centered or identical.

## Historical examples

### 1. Le Corbusier and Pierre Jeanneret, *Villa Savoye* (1928–1931)

- **Institution and direct source:** The Museum of Modern Art, [International Style collection overview](https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/international-style).
- **Image/viewing, credit, and reuse:** MoMA’s overview identifies its scale model and links collection records. Check the specific record for image rights before reproducing an image.
- **What I observe:** The house is raised on pilotis and organized through distinct horizontal planes, an open ground level, and ribbon windows. Its form communicates a systematic approach to space and construction.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** Use a strong horizontal structure and one repeated module, leaving open space for copy and a clear action.

### 2. Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, *Tugendhat House* (1928–1930)

- **Institution and direct source:** The Museum of Modern Art, [International Style collection overview](https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/international-style).
- **Image/viewing, credit, and reuse:** MoMA identifies the house’s scale model among its collection works. Use the object record as a viewing link and seek permission for image reproduction.
- **What I observe:** The house’s open plan, glass walls, and precise structural order connect interior space to its setting. Minimal ornament makes material, proportion, and light especially important.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** Use translucent layers or a restrained architectural grid, while maintaining strong text contrast and accessible reading sizes.
