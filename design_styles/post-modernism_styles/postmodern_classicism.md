# Postmodern Classicism

[Home](../README.md)

## What it is

Postmodern Classicism is an architectural approach that brings classical and vernacular references into contemporary buildings, often with changes in scale, material, or function that make the references playful or ambiguous. It developed as part of Postmodernism’s response to Modernism’s preference for abstract form and reduced ornament. The references are not always literal reconstruction: they can act as signs or commentary. [MoMA, Postmodernism](https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/postmodernism), [V&A, What is Postmodernism?](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/what-is-postmodernism).

## Recognize and apply it

- **Historical references:** Gables, columns, arches, pediments, and other familiar forms return in new settings. Choose references that connect to the place or message.
- **Changed scale or function:** Familiar motifs may be enlarged, flattened, or used decoratively rather than structurally. This creates surprise; keep the reference readable.
- **Layered meaning:** A building can be formal and playful at once, or combine classical cues with modern materials and planning. Use contrast with intention, not as random ornament.

In a poster or hero, a classical frame or arch can establish the reference, while contemporary typography and an unexpected color shift make the design feel current. Keep key information clear.

## Historical examples

### 1. Robert Venturi, *Vanna Venturi House, Chestnut Hill, Pennsylvania, Scheme VI (final)* (1959–1964)

- **Institution and direct source:** The Museum of Modern Art, [collection record](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/990).
- **Image/viewing, credit, and reuse:** MoMA provides an image of the scale model. The record credits it as a gift of Venturi, Rauch and Scott Brown, Inc.; the work is copyrighted. Link to view and obtain permission before reproducing the image.
- **What I observe:** The model uses familiar house signs—a gable, chimney, door, and windows—but combines them with altered symmetry and scale. The result reads as a house while complicating what a conventional house should look like.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** Use a recognizable frame or roofline as a graphic border, then alter its proportions to signal a fresh interpretation rather than a literal revival.

### 2. Michael Graves, *Fargo-Moorhead Cultural Center Bridge, project, south elevation* (1978)

- **Institution and direct source:** The Museum of Modern Art, [collection record](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/92).
- **Image/viewing, credit, and reuse:** MoMA provides an image of the drawing, credited to its collection (Lily Auchincloss Fund). The record is copyrighted; MoMA requests that image reproduction be licensed. Link for viewing and request permission before reuse.
- **What I observe:** Graves uses columns, arches, color, and displaced architectural details as visual signs for a civic bridge. The classical references are recognizable but altered, so the building’s meaning is not limited to structural function.
- **How I would adapt it in a hero:** Pair a recognizable architectural motif with an unexpected color or scale shift, and explain how that contrast relates to the offer.
